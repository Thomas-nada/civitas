**Vote: YES**

I am voting YES on PFMS because it backs a team that already has a government workflow live on Cardano, and it asks the treasury to fund the open, reusable part of taking that to state scale. This isn’t a vote that every projection in this proposal comes true. It’s a vote that, on the terms actually written into this action, the risk is bounded and the potential upside for Cardano justifies a measured bet.

Why I can support it. Trivolve has already done the hardest part of what most adoption proposals only promise: put a live government workflow on Cardano mainnet and kept it running. FMS has operated with a state police force for over a year. The structure is also stronger than most asks we see. The Cardano Foundation administers the funds, held in an account delegated to always-abstain. The proposal commits to releasing ₳7.8M of the ₳9.6M only after an independent assurer and the administrator verify milestones, and the Foundation now has to write that into its contract. It also caps spending at $0.30 per ADA, returns unspent funds, releases the core under Apache 2.0, and ties the final tranche to sustained live usage.

On “the government should pay.” This is the most serious objection, and DReps I respect have voted No on it. I think it frames the question slightly wrong. The treasury isn’t buying Telangana a police system. The state pays to run it: its infrastructure, its maintenance and every Cardano transaction fee. What the treasury buys is an open, reusable evidence-anchoring core, and the decision that the anchor lives on Cardano rather than on a government server or a proprietary vendor stack. Other ecosystems spend real money competing for exactly that kind of reference deployment. I’ve said before that when enterprises want something built, they should have skin in the game. Here the state pays to run the system and every fee, but nothing toward the build, so this only partly meets my own bar. I wish the proposal had separated reusable-core costs from state-specific work and asked the state to share in the build. That gap is real.

What gives me pause. ₳1.8M goes out at contract signing, before the assurer is appointed, and ₳5.8M before anything reaches mainnet. Transaction targets depend on a batching policy the vendor sets, and transaction counts aren’t value. FMS also fell well short of its own original forecasts. The 70/20/10 revenue share is a stated commitment, not yet an enforceable mechanism, so I’m not counting the revenue model in my decision. The state agreement is confidential and attested only by the Foundation, which is also co-author and administrator. A hash proves a record hasn’t changed since it was stamped; it doesn’t prove the record was true or lawfully collected. A system touching CCTV, ANPR and bodycam records at state scale deserves explicit privacy attention. And at roughly a fifth of our remaining Net Change Limit, the opportunity cost is real.

What I’m asking for. My vote can’t make these binding, so I’m asking the Foundation to put them in its contract with Trivolve and report on them publicly:

1.
Publish an attestation of the material terms of the state commitment.
2.
Contract the independent assurer directly and early, and publish every milestone report.
3.
Publish the batching policy at M1 and report coverage (live sources, evidence items, verifications by parties other than the operator) alongside transaction counts.
4.
Make the 70/20/10 split enforceable: defined revenue base, payment route, audit rights, and a term that covers future deployments.
5.
Keep standard verification free and independent of the operator for the accused, the defense and the courts.
6.
Confirm how multi-signature control applies to the receiving account, and complete a data-protection review.

The best part of this structure, if the Foundation contracts it as proposed, is that if Trivolve doesn’t deliver, the treasury stops paying. I expect the administrator to hold that line. On balance, I’m voting YES.
