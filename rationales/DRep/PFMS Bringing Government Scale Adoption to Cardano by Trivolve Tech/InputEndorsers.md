What if the problem is not primarily technological, but institutional - or maybe political will?

Why is the Cardano Treasury paying for software that a government wants? Was the Telangana government offered a conventional commercial development contract and, if so, what was its response?

Sometimes we see inefficiencies in systems and assume there must be a strong desire to eliminate them. We see a proposal claiming it could reduce criminal case resolution times by approximately 30%, and naturally think this would be welcomed by everyone.

But should we ask ourselves: is this a bug or a feature of the existing system?

If police records are managed by the same department that creates and uses them to prosecute crimes, is that simply a technical deficiency waiting to be fixed? Or does it reflect deeper institutional arrangements and interests? This can be changed. 

I am not suggesting that the authorities necessarily want these problems to persist. But if the existing arrangements are such a serious obstacle to justice, why have they not already been adequately addressed through legislation or existing technology?

The proposal identifies a trust gap in a state justice system. But addressing the underlying causes requires institutional commitment, not just better technology.

If the Telangana government really wants PFMS, why isn't it financing the development in full? Maybe its not so high up on the list of priorities. 

Are we really solving the underlying problem, or simply putting an expensive technological layer on top of a system whose fundamental problems may remain unchanged?
